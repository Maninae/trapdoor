Lost in the grid

Same dots, different arrows

Take whole numbers of steps along arrow A and arrow B, and you land on a dot. All the dots you can reach form a .

A lattice is a grid of points that repeats perfectly in every direction. Pick some arrows; the lattice is every point you reach by adding whole numbers of copies of them, backward steps included. Many different sets of arrows can build the same lattice.

Find the nearest dot

Now the dots are hidden. You get the arrows and a red point. Land on the dot closest to it. Try the tidy pair first, then the skewed one.

Hundreds of directions

With short, nearly square arrows, a shortcut works: write the red point as a recipe and round each number. With long, skewed arrows, rounding lands far off, and every step leaps across the page.

On paper you can just look. A real lattice has hundreds of , and there is nothing to look at. Given only skewed arrows, nobody knows how to find the nearest dot quickly. As far as anyone knows, that includes a quantum computer: the hidden repeating pattern from chapter 8 does not seem to be there.

Each dimension is one more independent direction. A page has two, a room has three. Math happily allows 500: a point is then a list of 500 numbers, and an arrow moves all of them at once.

One of the earliest designs (1997) used exactly this trapdoor. The tidy arrows were the private key; the skewed arrows were the public key.

Small errors

In 2005 Oded Regev found a sturdier way to hide a secret. Write clock equations in some secret numbers, then nudge every answer by a tiny random amount. This is .

Learning with errors asks you to recover secret numbers from many clock equations whose answers are each slightly wrong. Regev proved that, for a quantum computer, solving it is at least as hard as certain worst-case lattice problems. Nearly all lattice cryptography in use today builds on it.

Learning with errors on Wikipedia

Without the nudge, school elimination solves the equations at once. With it, the errors grow as you eliminate.

Now the standard

In August 2024 NIST, the US standards body, published two lattice standards built on learning with errors: ML-KEM for agreeing on a key and ML-DSA for signatures. The price is size, kilobytes where curves need dozens of bytes.
