Trapdoor Chapter 6

Signatures

Who sent this?

When you open your bank's website, your browser needs proof that it really is the bank and not an impostor in the middle. The proof is a : a calculation only the private-key holder can do, which anyone can check with the public key.

A digital signature ties a message to one key holder. Unlike a handwritten one, it is different for every message, so it cannot be cut out and pasted onto another. Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman proposed the idea in 1976, and RSA made it work soon after.

Digital signature on Wikipedia

It runs Bob's keys from chapter 5 in reverse. To sign, Bob raises a number to his private power, 2753, on the 3233-hour clock. To check, anyone raises the signature to the public power, 17. The number comes back to where it started only if 2753 was used.

Sign the fingerprint

Bob does not sign the message itself, which could be any length. He signs its hash, the fingerprint from chapter 2. Change one letter and the fingerprint changes, so the old signature no longer fits.

Our toy squeezes the fingerprint onto a 3233-hour clock, so a forger could get lucky 1 time in 3233. Real signatures use keys of 2048 bits or more, where luck is hopeless.

The padlock is a chain of signatures

How does your browser learn the bank's public key? The bank sends a certificate: its name and public key, signed by a . That authority's key is signed by another, up to a root authority whose key came built into your browser or phone.

A certificate authority is an organization that checks who owns a website before signing its certificate. Browsers and operating systems ship with a short list of root authorities they trust. Let's Encrypt is one that signs certificates for free.
