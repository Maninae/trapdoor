Peter Shor showed in 1994 that a quantum computer could find the hidden repeating pattern inside factoring and curve problems, and so solve them quickly. No machine big enough to run it on real key sizes exists yet.

A cautionary tale

SIKE rested on a newer problem: finding a path between two elliptic curves, called an . In July 2022 NIST kept it for a fourth round of review. Weeks later, Wouter Castryck and Thomas Decru broke it on an ordinary computer, recovering a key for the smallest setting in about an hour on one processor core. No quantum computer needed.

An isogeny is a map from one elliptic curve to another that respects the curves' addition rule. SIKE hid a secret in a long chain of such maps. The 2022 attack used extra public information SIKE handed out to rebuild the chain.

New math needs years of clever people attacking it before anyone should trust it.

Belt and braces

So the web hedges. A browser can do the key agreement twice, once with X25519 and once with ML-KEM-768, and mix the two results. This hybrid, X25519MLKEM768, stays safe as long as either half holds.

Recent Chrome, Edge and Firefox offer it by default, as do Apple's 2025 systems. It is used only when the website's server supports it too.

Structure is what makes a one-way street possible: a clock that wraps, a curve, a grid. It is also what a clever solver grabs onto. Every trapdoor here is a bet that nobody has found the handle.
