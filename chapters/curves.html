Hopping on a curve

A strange way to add

The curve is every point where y2 = x3 − x + 1. Pick two points on it. The line through them crosses the curve once more. Flip that crossing over the x-axis, and you have P + Q.

A vertical line never crosses a third time. Its answer is an extra point called .

Mathematicians add one point that sits "at infinity", where every vertical line meets. It acts like 0 in ordinary adding: P + zero = P. A point plus its mirror image across the x-axis gives zero.

Point at infinity on Wikipedia

Now add P to itself again and again: 2P = P + P, then 3P = 2P + P. To add P to itself, use the line that just touches the curve at P. Each hop is one use of the rule.

The same curve on a clock

Now keep only whole numbers, and wrap both x and y around a clock, as in clock math. The curve turns into a scatter of dots. The adding rule still works, with .

The rule needs the slope of a line, which means dividing. On a prime clock, dividing by 2 means multiplying by the number that turns 2 into 1. On a 29-hour clock that is 15, because 2 × 15 = 30 lands on 1.

Easy forward, hard backward

Hopping forward is quick. To reach 1,000,000P you do not hop a million times. Double P to 2P, 4P, 8P, and so on, then add up the ones you need: 25 steps.

Backward is the hard part. Shown the start dot and the end dot, how many hops? On a well-chosen curve, nobody knows a fast way.

Same strength, smaller keys

Curve key
256 bits
Two-key system
3,072 bits

US standards body NIST rates a 256- curve key about as strong as a 3,072-bit key in the two-key system. Smaller keys mean less to send and less to compute, which is why phones and browsers often prefer curves.

A bit is one binary digit, a 0 or a 1. A 256-bit number has up to 256 binary digits, which is about 78 ordinary digits.

Clock powers and curve hops both have a hidden rhythm: keep going and you come back to the start.
