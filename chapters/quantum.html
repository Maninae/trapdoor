The rhythm finder

Every power has a rhythm

Raise a number to higher and higher powers on a clock, as in clock math, and the landings repeat. The repeat length is the .

The period is the smallest number of steps after which a pattern starts over. Powers of 7 on a 15-hour clock go 1, 7, 4, 13, 1, 7, 4, 13, … so the period is 4.

The period splits the clock

Here is the surprise. Powers of 7 on a 15-hour clock have period 4. Half of 4 is 2, and 72 = 49 lands on 4. Now 4 − 1 = 3 and 4 + 1 = 5 each share a factor with 15, and a pulls it out: 15 = 3 × 5.

The greatest common divisor of two numbers is the biggest number that divides both. Euclid's method finds it fast: divide, keep the remainder, repeat. The last nonzero remainder is the answer.

Sometimes you are unlucky: the period is odd, or the half power lands on one less than the clock size. Then try another number. For clocks like these, at least half the choices work.

Finding the period fast

Splitting the clock size breaks the two-key system. A cousin of the same trick finds the secret powers of the shared secret and the hop counts of curves.

An ordinary computer steps through powers one at a time. With hundreds of digits, the period is far out of reach, and even the cleverest known ordinary methods are too slow. In 1994 Peter Shor found a method for a that finds the period fast. It sets up waves for every possible rhythm at once, so the wrong ones cancel and the true one adds up.

A quantum computer stores information in qubits, which can hold a blend of 0 and 1 at once. Its steps act on all the possibilities together, and the possibilities interfere like waves. It is not just a faster ordinary computer; it only helps on problems with special structure.

Not yet

No quantum computer is anywhere near big enough. On real hardware, Shor's algorithm has only factored tiny numbers such as 15 and 21, and those runs leaned on already knowing the answer. People worry now anyway, because messages recorded today could be read once a big machine exists.

Scrambling has no rhythm

The ciphers and fingerprints of scrambling have no period to find. The best known quantum attack on them, Grover's algorithm (1996), only takes a off a brute-force search. The remedy there is simply longer keys.

A search through 2128 possible keys would take a quantum computer about 264 steps, the square root. Doubling the key length to 256 bits puts the work back at 2128.
