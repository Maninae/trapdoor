Trapdoor Chapter 5

Two keys

Easy to multiply, slow to split

Multiplying two takes a computer a blink. Going back, from the product to the two primes, has no known shortcut anywhere near as quick.

A prime has no divisors except 1 and itself. Every whole number above 1 splits into primes in exactly one way, like 3233 = 53 × 61. Finding that split is called factoring.

Integer factorization on Wikipedia

The plain method divides by 2, 3, 5, 7, and onward until one goes in evenly. Two more digits in the product make that search about ten times longer.

A lock anyone can close

Bob picks two primes, 61 and 53, and publishes their product, 3233, with the number 17. That pair is his public key: an open padlock anyone can snap shut. To lock a number, raise it to the 17th power on a 3233-hour clock.

Bob keeps a second number, 2753, as his . Raising the locked number to the 2753rd power on the same clock brings back the original.

Bob finds 2753 from his primes. He multiplies 61 − 1 by 53 − 1 to get 3120, then looks for a number that times 17 lands on 1 on a 3120-hour clock: 17 × 2753 = 46,801 = 15 × 3120 + 1. Without 61 and 53, nobody can get 3120.

RSA worked example on Wikipedia

Why Eve is stuck

Anyone who splits 3233 can rebuild 2753. Here that takes a few tries. A real public key is a number, about 617 digits long, and nobody knows how to split one in any reasonable time.

Bits count binary digits, so a 2048-bit number is about 22048, which has 617 ordinary digits. In 2020, splitting a 250-digit challenge number called RSA-250 took about 2,700 years of computer-core time.

RSA numbers on Wikipedia

Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman published this system, RSA, in 1977. Clifford Cocks had found it at GCHQ, the British spy agency, in 1973, but that stayed secret until 1997.

If anyone can lock a message to you, how do you know who sent it?
