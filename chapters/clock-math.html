Clock math

Counting that wraps around

On a 12-hour clock, 15 o'clock is 3 o'clock. You go around once and have 3 hours left over. That leftover is the after dividing by 12.

When a division does not come out even, the remainder is what is left over. 15 divided by 12 is 1, with 3 left over. The remainder is always smaller than the number you divided by.

Remainder on Wikipedia

We write this as 15 12 = 3. Our clocks put 0 at the top where 12 would be, so a clock with n hours shows 0 through n − 1.

Short for "modulo". a mod n means the remainder when a is divided by n, which is the hour where a lands on an n-hour clock. Many programming languages write it as a % n.

Adding and multiplying

Clock arithmetic uses the rules you already know. Add or multiply as usual, then wrap. On a 12-hour clock, 9 + 5 = 14, which lands on 2.

You can also wrap each number first and land in the same place. That keeps the numbers small, which matters in a moment.

Powers on a prime clock

Now multiply by the same number again and again. Take a clock whose number of hours is , say 7. The powers of 3 are 3, 9, 27, 81, and so on. They land on 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1, and then the pattern repeats.

A prime is a whole number above 1 that only 1 and itself divide evenly. 7, 17, and 29 are prime. 12 is not, because 12 = 3 × 4.

Prime number on Wikipedia

Each hop multiplies the last landing and wraps. On a bigger clock, watch how the path jumps around.

Easy forward, hard backward

Going forward is quick. Given the power, a short chain of multiplications tells you where it lands, even on an enormous clock.

Going backward is slow. Told only where it landed, can you find which power got there? The hops look scrambled, so the obvious method is to try powers one at a time.

This forward-easy, backward-hard property is the trapdoor that later lessons build on.
