A secret in plain sight

Mixing in public

Alice and Bob have never met. Eve hears every word they say. Still, they end up holding the same color, and Eve does not.

The trick is that mixing paint is easy and unmixing it is not. Eve sees both mixtures, but she cannot pull a secret color back out of either one. Pouring them together gives her too much yellow.

The same trick with powers

Now swap paint for powers on a prime clock, from chapter 3. Everyone knows the clock and a start number. Alice picks a secret power and announces where the start number lands. Bob does the same with his own power.

Then each raises the other's announced hour to their own secret power. Both have now computed the start number to the power Alice's times Bob's, a , so they land on the same hour.

Raising 54 to the 3rd power multiplies three copies of 54, which is twelve 5s: (54)3 = 54×3. Swapping the order gives (53)4, also twelve 5s. Wrapping on a clock does not change this.

What Eve is missing

Eve hears the clock, the start number, and both announced hours. To reach the shared hour she needs one of the secret powers, which means working a power backward. That is the slow direction.

Real clocks have hundreds of digits; a common one is a prime with 617 digits. Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published this in 1976.

A key is a secret number that scrambles and unscrambles messages, as in chapter 2. A key exchange is a way for two people to end up with the same key without ever sending it. At GCHQ, the British spy agency, Malcolm Williamson had found the same method around 1974, but it stayed secret until 1997.
